Remembering · List and identify

Characteristics and five generations of computers

Four primary characteristics

Input→Processing→Output
  • Speed: performs millions or billions of operations rapidly.
  • Accuracy: produces precise results when data and instructions are correct.
  • Diligence: repeats tasks without tiring or losing consistency.
  • Storage: stores large amounts of data and retrieves it when required.

Other commonly described characteristics include automation, versatility, and reliability.

Understanding · Explain

Functional block diagram of a computer

Input unit→Main memory↔CPU→Output unit

CPU contains the Control Unit (CU), Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU), and registers. Storage communicates with memory and the system through suitable interfaces.

  • Input unit: accepts data and instructions through devices such as a keyboard or scanner and converts them into machine-readable form.
  • Memory unit: holds instructions, input data, intermediate results, and final results. Primary memory includes RAM and ROM.
  • CPU: the CU directs and coordinates operations; the ALU performs arithmetic and logical comparisons; registers hold data temporarily for rapid access.
  • Output unit: presents results through a monitor, printer, speaker, or other output device.
  • System buses and I/O interfaces: carry data, address information, and control signals between components. The CU coordinates read/write operations and device activity.

Working: data and instructions enter through input, are stored in memory, and are fetched and executed by the CPU. Results may be stored again or sent to an output device. Control signals coordinate each step.

Applying · Demonstrate

Using scanners and card readers on campus

Attendance workflow

Student ID / QR→Scanner or RFID→Attendance database
  • Assign each student a unique barcode, QR code, RFID card, or smart card.
  • At the classroom entrance, scan the ID or tap the card at a reader.
  • The system verifies the ID and records the date, time, and class.
  • Authorized staff review attendance reports and correct genuine errors.

Library checkout workflow

Book barcode+Student card→Library system
  • Scan the book barcode and student card, or read an RFID tag.
  • The library system checks the member and book record.
  • It saves the checkout date and due date, and updates availability.
  • Return scans update the record and can support overdue reminders.

Benefits: faster records, fewer manual entry errors, easier reporting, and better tracking. Precautions: protect student data, restrict staff access, keep a manual backup, and provide a correction process for failed or duplicate scans.

Evaluating · Assess and justify

Plotter versus UHD monitor and large-format printer

CriterionDrum / flatbed plotterUHD monitor + large-format printer
Line workTraditional pen plotters can draw continuous precise lines; modern plotters may use other technologies.High-resolution display helps inspect detail; printer resolution and media quality determine the final print.
ScalabilityDepends on plotter bed/media width and model.Digital drawings can be scaled in CAD; print size is limited by printer and roll/sheet width.
WorkflowSpecialized direct drawing; older pen plotters are less common today.Review and revise on screen, then print multiple sheets or revisions.
CostSpecialized equipment may have higher maintenance or consumable costs.Requires both display and printer; costs include ink/toner, media, calibration, and maintenance.
CAD file→UHD review→Large-format print

Assessment: for most modern civil-engineering offices, a high-quality CAD workstation plus a reliable large-format printer is the more flexible general-purpose choice because drawings can be reviewed, revised, archived, and reproduced. A specialized plotter may be preferable where a particular workflow demands it. Final selection should compare required line quality, sheet size, throughput, reliability, and total cost over the equipment’s life; “UHD” alone does not determine printed accuracy.

Evaluating · Critique

Facial recognition versus voice recognition for authentication

FactorFacial recognitionVoice recognition
High ambient noiseUsually unaffected by sound, but needs a usable camera view and adequate lighting.Noise can mask speech and reduce accuracy; microphones and noise cancellation may help.
Public exposureCan be captured without deliberate speech; raises consent and surveillance concerns.Can be overheard or recorded; public noise may cause errors and spoken phrases may be replayed.
Presentation attacksPhotos, screens, masks, or deepfakes may be threats; liveness checks help but are not perfect.Recordings or synthetic voices may be threats; challenge phrases and anti-spoofing help but are not perfect.
AccessibilityMay be difficult with camera placement, facial changes, or visual conditions.May be difficult for users with speech differences or in noisy environments.
Privacy and biasRequires careful handling of biometric templates and testing across demographic groups.Voice data also requires protection and testing across accents, languages, and speech patterns.

Critique: in a noisy public setting, facial recognition may be more practical than voice recognition if lighting and camera placement are suitable, but it should not be treated as infallible. Both systems can fail or be spoofed. Use informed notice, secure storage, liveness/anti-spoofing controls, rate limits, human review, and an alternative authentication method. For sensitive access, combine factors rather than relying on one biometric alone.

Creating · Design

Accessible hands-free workstation for a visually impaired user

Voice recognition→Computer + accessible OS→Screen reader / speech output
Tactile keyboard / braille input↔Computer→Refreshable Braille display (optional)

Proposed components and purpose

  • Voice recognition: supports hands-free dictation, launching apps, navigation, and commands.
  • Screen reader and voice response synthesizer: reads menus, documents, buttons, notifications, and form labels aloud.
  • Tactile keyboard: raised or clearly distinguishable keys provide an alternative for precise text entry; it is not hands-free, so it should be optional rather than the only input method.
  • Refreshable Braille display (optional): presents text as tactile Braille for users who read Braille.
  • Accessible software: use keyboard-accessible controls, meaningful labels, logical focus order, adjustable speech rate, and shortcuts.
  • Physical setup: keep cables tidy, provide comfortable headset/microphone placement, and make power and volume controls easy to locate.
  • Privacy and safety: use headphones where appropriate, secure voice data, confirm important commands before execution, and provide a non-voice fallback.

Example workflow: the user says “Open my assignment.” Voice recognition opens the document; the screen reader announces its title and reads the content; the user dictates edits; speech output confirms actions. A tactile keyboard or Braille display provides an alternative whenever needed.

Conclusion

Computers combine input, processing, memory, and output to perform tasks efficiently. Understanding hardware generations, languages, and input/output devices helps users select suitable systems. In real deployments, reliability, privacy, accessibility, maintenance, and total cost should be considered alongside technical performance.
